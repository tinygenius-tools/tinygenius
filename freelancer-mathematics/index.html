Freelance Pricing in 2027–2028: Calculate Your Rate, Protect Your Profit and Negotiate Better

Seven practical calculations to help freelancers and independent creators price their work, understand what they actually keep and negotiate scope, payment and usage rights.

Freelance pricing is not simply choosing an hourly number and hoping clients accept it. A sustainable price has to account for the time you can actually bill, the costs of running your business, the work included in a project and the commercial rights a client receives.

This guide is designed for planning in 2027–2028. Its worked examples are hypothetical calculations, not forecasts or claims about future market rates. Your price depends on your service, experience, location, client type, costs, tax position and the exact agreement. Recheck current market evidence when quoting; a future year in a headline does not make historical data current.

1. Calculate the hourly rate your business needs

Start with the annual revenue your business must generate. Include the personal income you need, business expenses and a reasonable buffer. Divide that target by realistic billable hours—not every hour you spend working.

Baseline hourly rate = Required annual revenue ÷ Annual billable hours

Worked example: assume you need $48,000 for personal income, $12,000 for business expenses and a $6,000 buffer. Your annual revenue target is $66,000.

AssumptionCalculationResult
Working time40 hours × 46 weeks1,840 hours
Billable capacity1,840 × 60%1,104 hours
Required revenue$48,000 + $12,000 + $6,000$66,000
Baseline rate$66,000 ÷ 1,104$59.78/hour

The 60% billable assumption is an illustration, not a universal rule. Upwork's 2026 rate-setting guidance also recommends allowing for non-billable time and business expenses; it describes 60% billable time as a typical starting point. See Upwork's rate-setting guidance.

This is a planning baseline, not a guaranteed take-home amount or a market quote. Check whether taxes, platform fees, insurance, payment costs and other expenses are already included. Do not count any cost twice. Use the Freelance Rate Calculator to test your own assumptions and read our step-by-step hourly-rate guide.

2. Stress-test your billable hours

Your rate becomes fragile if it only works when every week is full. Compare scenarios before setting a minimum.

These are scenario calculations, not predictions. A lower billable share may reflect time spent finding clients, managing projects, learning, invoicing or dealing with gaps between contracts. Track your own hours over several weeks and replace assumptions with observed data.

3. Choose the right pricing model

  • Hourly: useful when the workload is uncertain, ongoing or likely to change.
  • Fixed project price: useful when deliverables, responsibilities, revisions and acceptance criteria are defined.
  • Value-based: potentially suitable when you can credibly connect your work to a measurable client outcome. Do not promise results you cannot control.

A fixed price should be built from a task estimate, not a guess. Break the work into research, preparation, production, communication, revisions, quality checks and handover. Upwork's fixed-price project estimation guide similarly emphasizes defining scope, estimating tasks and protecting against scope creep.

Project quote = Estimated labour + direct costs + allocated overhead + risk allowance

A planning framework, not a universal accounting formula. Make sure each cost is included only once.

4. Calculate what remains after project costs

Revenue is not the same as profit. The following example shows the amount remaining after only the listed fees and direct costs; it is not net profit because overhead and taxes have not yet been deducted.

Profit margin is profit divided by revenue. Markup is profit divided by cost; these percentages are not interchangeable. Use the Profit Margin Calculator to model costs, target prices, margins and break-even points using your own figures.

5. Price the full scope—not just the visible deliverable

A quote should explain exactly what the client receives. State deliverables, formats, milestones, deadlines, client dependencies, included revision rounds and what counts as additional work. If the client adds a new deliverable, changes the brief or requests extra rounds, estimate the additional time and agree a revised fee, scope or timeline.

For practical examples, read how to handle scope creep and how to respond when a client asks for a discount.

6. Treat UGC usage rights as a separate commercial decision

Producing a video or image is not the same as granting every possible right to use it. A creator's quotation may need to specify organic social use, paid advertising, platforms, licence duration, territory, exclusivity, raw footage, editing permissions and renewal terms.

A limited organic-use licence and perpetual paid advertising rights are different arrangements. If a brand wants broader usage, discuss a separate licence fee, a shorter initial term, narrower platforms or another explicit trade-off. There is no universal add-on price that fits every creator or campaign.

7. Compare offers by effective return and negotiation terms

The largest headline payment is not automatically the best deal. Compare the money remaining after known fees and direct costs with the hours required. Then assess payment reliability, timing, client communication, deadline pressure, risk and future opportunity.

Offer AOffer B
Payment$1,000$800
Fees and direct costs$100$40
Remaining before overhead/tax$900$760
Estimated hours4020
Effective return per hour$22.50$38.00

Offer B has the higher effective return per hour in this hypothetical example. That does not automatically make it the better agreement: payment risk, workload, strategic value and contract terms still matter.

Know your BATNA and your minimum acceptable terms

BATNA means your best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Identify realistic alternatives before deciding whether to accept, reject or counter. An unconfirmed lead is not guaranteed income. Consider your reservation point—the minimum acceptable outcome—and whether a ZOPA (zone of possible agreement) may exist between the parties.

Exchange concessions instead of discounting blindly

If a client cannot meet your price, consider fewer deliverables, fewer revisions, a longer timeline, a shorter usage licence, reduced exclusivity or staged work. Make the exchange explicit: if the price changes, what else changes? Avoid reducing the fee while leaving the same scope, deadline and rights untouched unless you have a clear commercial reason.

Your freelance pricing checklist

  • Have I separated billable time from total working time?
  • Does my revenue target include the costs and buffer I actually need?
  • Have I stress-tested a lower billable-capacity scenario?
  • Have I estimated all tasks, communication and revisions?
  • Are deliverables, deadlines and exclusions written clearly?
  • Have I checked usage rights, duration and exclusivity?
  • Have I compared effective return, payment risk and my alternatives?
  • Does every discount or concession have a clear reason?

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my freelance hourly rate?

Add the annual income you need, relevant business costs and an appropriate buffer. Divide that revenue target by realistic annual billable hours, then check taxes and fees that may not yet be included.

What percentage of freelance time should be billable?

There is no fixed percentage. A 60% assumption is sometimes used as a starting point, but your actual share depends on client acquisition, administration, project management and the nature of your work. Track your own time.

Should I charge hourly or by project?

Hourly pricing can suit uncertain or ongoing work. Fixed pricing can suit well-defined deliverables. Choose the structure that makes scope, risk and payment clear to both parties.

How do I price additional revisions?

State how many revision rounds are included. For additional rounds or a changed brief, estimate the added work and agree a fee, revised scope or timeline before proceeding.

Should UGC creators charge separately for usage rights?

Creators should define usage permissions clearly. Paid advertising, licence duration, platforms, territory, exclusivity and raw footage can materially change the commercial arrangement.

How can I negotiate when a client says my price is too high?

Ask what constraint is driving the objection. Consider changing scope, timing, deliverables or usage rights rather than automatically discounting the same work.
