Client Asked for a Discount? 5 Fair Counteroffer Strategies

A client likes your proposal but says the price is too high. You want the project, but you do not want to accept the same workload for less. The strongest response is usually an exchange of terms—not an automatic discount.

TinyGenius Editorial Team · 19 November 2026 · 7-minute read

The short answer: acknowledge the budget concern, clarify what is constrained, protect your minimum acceptable outcome, then offer a lower-cost version only when something changes in return: scope, rights, timeline, payment risk or commitment.

A discount request is information, not an instruction. It may reflect a real budget limit, uncertainty about value or a comparison with another provider. Understand the problem before choosing a response.

1. Know your three numbers

  • Opening quote: price for the defined deliverables and terms.
  • Target outcome: the agreement you would be pleased to accept.
  • Reservation point: the least attractive combination of price and terms you are willing to accept, considering your realistic alternatives.

Your minimum is not only a dollar amount. A shorter license, smaller scope or more reliable payment can make a lower fee workable. Check estimated effort, costs, capacity, payment risk and rights before replying.

2. Find out what is actually constrained

Ask: “Is the main constraint the total budget, or is there a particular part of the scope or timing you would like us to adjust?”

If the budget is fixed, reduce or phase the work. If the client doubts the value, explain the deliverables and process. If they are comparing quotes, compare what each includes rather than racing to the lowest number.

3. Trade terms instead of giving away value

Client wantsPossible counteroffer
Lower total feeReduce deliverables or phase the work
Same assets for lessShorten paid usage, limit territory or platforms, exclude whitelisting
Lower price nowConsider payment terms that genuinely reduce cash-flow risk
More work within budgetOffer a smaller first phase and price the extension separately
Ongoing discountRequire defined volume, duration and cancellation terms

For UGC deals, separate the creation fee from the license. Paid advertising, creator-handle authorization, exclusivity, raw footage, duration and territory are distinct commercial variables. A lower production fee should not silently grant broader rights.

4. Compare options—not just prices

Illustrative example, not a market benchmark:

Your proposal is $1,200 for four edited videos, one revision round and 90 days of paid usage. The client asks for $900.

  • Option A: $1,200 for the original scope and rights.
  • Option B: $900 for three videos, with the same 90-day usage.
  • Option C: $900 for four videos but only 30 days of paid usage; extensions are quoted separately.

Compare production cost, license value, capacity and the client's objective. None of these options is automatically fair without checking the facts.

5. Use BATNA and ZOPA—not pressure

BATNA is your best alternative if this deal does not happen. It may be another project, time to find a better-fit client or capacity reserved for existing work. Be realistic: an uncertain lead is not a signed contract.

ZOPA is the zone where both sides' acceptable outcomes overlap. You may not know the client's maximum budget, and they may not know your minimum. Explore trades that create value before concluding there is no overlap.

Game theory reminds us that both parties act on their own incentives and alternatives. A durable agreement should make the trade-offs clear rather than rely on one side misunderstanding the terms.

6. A client-ready counteroffer

Thanks for being open about the budget. I understand the need to keep the project within a workable range.

The current proposal covers [deliverables], [revision allowance] and [usage terms]. Rather than reduce the fee while keeping every element unchanged, I can offer two alternatives:

  1. [Adjusted scope] for [revised fee], delivered by [date].
  2. [Original scope with adjusted rights, timeline or payment terms] for [revised fee], with [specific limits].

Which option is closer to your priorities? I can confirm the revised scope in writing before we begin.

Be specific. Avoid false deadlines, invented competing offers or claims about being fully booked if they are not true. Credibility matters in repeat business.

7. Know when to decline

Declining can be rational when the fee falls below your sustainable minimum, scope cannot be bounded, payment risk is unacceptable, rights requested are disproportionate or the work would displace a better alternative. Consider the whole package—not just the headline amount.

Fair & Just principle: a discount is a change in the deal, not a reason to make work invisible. If the price changes, make the corresponding trade-off visible too.

Frequently asked questions

Should I ever give a client a discount?

Yes, when there is a clear commercial reason and you can afford the trade. Smaller scope, predictable volume, reduced rights or lower payment risk may justify a different price.

How much should I reduce my price?

There is no universal percentage. Calculate the cost and value of the work being removed or changed. Avoid arbitrary discount rules.

What if another freelancer is cheaper?

Compare deliverables, quality, timeline, revision allowance and rights. Explain differences without criticizing the other provider.

What if the client wants the same scope for less?

Hold the original quote, explain its value, or offer a different scope. You are not required to accept terms that do not work for your business.
